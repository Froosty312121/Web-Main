On the northwestern edge of Milan, three towers twist toward the sky like sculptural objects rendered at urban scale. They are the most visible symbols of CityLife — Milan's most ambitious urban transformation project of the 21st century. Rising from the site of the city's former fairgrounds, this new district represents a bold vision of what a modern European city can be: a place where architecture, green space, commerce, and residential life coexist in a carefully planned ecosystem. And at its center lies a 17-hectare park that is the largest new green space Milan has created in over a century.

From Fiera to Future

The story of CityLife begins with Milan's old fairgrounds — the Fieramilanocity, built in 1923 on a 365,000-square-meter site in the neighborhood of Portello. For decades, this was Milan's primary exhibition space, hosting trade shows, cultural events, and the famous Milan Furniture Fair (Salone del Mobile). But by the 1990s, the fairgrounds had become too small for Milan's growing needs, and a decision was made to relocate the exhibition to a new, larger site in Rho on the city's periphery.

This left the city with an enormous question: what to do with 365,000 square meters of prime real estate in the heart of Milan? The answer was CityLife — a master plan to transform the former fairgrounds into a mixed-use district featuring residential towers, office buildings, a shopping center, and most importantly, a large public park.

"CityLife is not just a real estate development — it is Milan's statement to the world that it remains one of Europe's great cities, capable of reinventing itself while respecting its past." — Stefano Boeri, architect and urbanist

Three Architects, Three Visions

What makes CityLife extraordinary is not just its scale but the caliber of its architects. The city's planners made the bold decision to commission three of the world's most celebrated architects, each designing a distinctive residential or office tower. The result is a trio of buildings that are as different from each other as they are from anything else in Milan's skyline.

Torre Isozaki — "The Straight Tower"

Japanese architect Arata Isozaki designed the Allianz Tower, also known affectionately as "il Dritto" (the Straight One). Rising 209 meters (though the habitable floors reach 175 meters), it is the tallest building in Italy. Its clean, rectangular form belies a complex internal structure, with a core of reinforced concrete and an outer skin of glass and aluminum. The tower's simplicity of form is its strength — in a skyline of curves and twists, the Straight Tower stands as a declaration of architectural clarity.

Torre Hadid — "The Twisting Tower"

The Generali Tower, designed by the late Zaha Hadid, is perhaps the most visually striking of the three. Its undulating form twists as it rises, creating a sense of movement that seems almost organic. The tower's facade ripples with horizontal bands of windows and cladding, giving the impression of a building in motion. Hadid, known for her fluid, dynamic designs, called the tower "a vertical city" that would house offices and apartments within its twisting envelope.

Torre Libeskind — "The Curving Tower"

Daniel Libeskind's residential tower, known as "il Curvo" (the Curved One), completes the trio. Unlike the other two towers, which are primarily office buildings, the Libeskind tower contains luxury apartments. Its curved form creates a dialogue with the circular geometry of the park below, and its balconies offer panoramic views across Milan to the Alps on clear days.

The Park: Milan's Newest Green Lung

At the heart of CityLife lies its most important public amenity: the Parco CityLife, a 17-hectare park designed by the landscape architecture firm Gustafson Porter + Bowman. The park is not an afterthought or a decorative element — it is the organizing principle of the entire development. Buildings are arranged around the park, roads curve to preserve its boundaries, and public life radiates outward from its green center.

The park's design features undulating lawns, a large central lake, winding paths, and over 2,000 newly planted trees. It preserves and integrates many of the mature trees that were already on the site from the old fairgrounds, creating a sense of established greenery even in a brand-new landscape. The park also houses several playgrounds, a dog park, and outdoor fitness areas.

Preserving History

CityLife was not built on empty land. The old Fieramilanocity fairgrounds had been a fixture of Milanese life for over 80 years, and the redevelopment team made the decision to preserve several historically significant structures from the original complex. The most notable is the Torre del Parco (Tower of the Park), a 1930s structure that has been renovated and integrated into the new development.

The preservation of these buildings reflects a philosophy that CityLife is not a tabula rasa but a palimpsest — a new layer written on top of the old, acknowledging the history of the site while building its future.

Parco CityLife opens to the public, providing 17 hectares of green space to the neighborhood.
2020s
The remaining towers and buildings are completed. CityLife becomes a functioning, vibrant new district of Milan.

Sustainability and Innovation

CityLife was designed from the outset as a sustainable development. The buildings incorporate advanced energy systems, green roofs, and high-performance facades that minimize energy consumption. The district is served by an extended metro line (the M5 lilac line), reducing car dependency. The park's lake serves as a rainwater retention basin, and the district's heating and cooling systems use geothermal energy.

The development also includes affordable housing units, a requirement that was controversial during the planning process but ultimately included to ensure that CityLife would be a socially mixed community rather than an exclusive enclave. The district aims to house approximately 10,000 residents and provide workspace for 16,000 people.

A New Chapter for Milan

CityLife represents a new chapter in Milan's long history of urban innovation. From the Roman Mediolanum to the medieval communes, from the Sforza Renaissance to the industrial revolution, Milan has always been a city that builds forward. CityLife continues that tradition — transforming an obsolete industrial site into a living, breathing neighborhood that combines architecture, nature, commerce, and residential life.

Whether you come to admire the twisting towers, stroll through the park, shop in the Fuksas-designed mall, or simply watch the Milanese sunset paint the glass facades in shades of gold and orange, CityLife offers a glimpse into what the cities of the future might look like — ambitious, green, and unapologetically beautiful.
