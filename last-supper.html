In a dim refectory on the western edge of Milan, Leonardo da Vinci painted a miracle. His "Last Supper" — Il Cenacolo — captures the precise moment when Christ tells his disciples that one of them will betray him. In that frozen instant, Leonardo revolutionized the art of narrative painting. Each apostle reacts with a different expression of shock, denial, or horror. It is a psychological drama painted on a wall, and it has captivated viewers for more than five centuries.

The Commission

The story begins with Ludovico Sforza, Duke of Milan, known as "il Moro." Around 1494, Ludovico commissioned Leonardo to decorate the refectory (dining hall) of the convent adjoining the church of Santa Maria delle Grazie. The Duke intended the church to serve as the Sforza family mausoleum, and he wanted the refectory to be adorned with a painting worthy of such a prestigious setting.

Leonardo was already well-known in Milan, having arrived at the Sforza court in 1482. He had painted portraits, designed festival decorations, and advised on military and civil engineering projects. But the Last Supper would be his most ambitious public work — and one that would test his artistic innovation to its limits.

"Leonardo did not paint a scene — he painted a moment. The Last Supper is not about what happened, but about what was felt when it happened." — Pietro Marani, Leonardo scholar

A Revolutionary Technique

The most fateful decision Leonardo made was to reject the traditional fresco technique. In fresco painting, the artist applies pigment to wet plaster, creating a durable, long-lasting image. But fresco requires speed and limits the artist's ability to rework details — qualities that Leonardo, with his obsessive perfectionism, found intolerable.

Instead, Leonardo developed an experimental technique: he applied a ground of lead white and gesso to the dry wall, then painted with oil and tempera binders, much as he would on a wooden panel. This allowed him to work slowly, layer by layer, blending colors and refining details over months and years. It also meant that the paint had no chemical bond with the wall beneath it — a choice that would have devastating consequences.

The Composition

Leonardo organized his thirteen figures into four groups of three, each group responding to Christ's announcement with a distinct emotional register. At the center, Christ sits in serene isolation, his hands spread in acceptance. To his right and left, the apostles ripple outward in waves of agitation — some leaning forward in shock, others recoiling, some arguing among themselves about who the traitor might be.

Judas, traditionally depicted on the opposite side of the table in medieval versions of the scene, is placed among the other apostles. His face is in shadow, his hand clutching a money bag, but he is not visually separated from the others. This psychological subtlety — the idea that betrayal can sit at the same table as loyalty — was revolutionary.

Five Centuries of Decay

The mural began deteriorating within Leonardo's own lifetime. By 1517 — less than 20 years after its completion — visitors noted that it was already fading. The experimental technique had failed to bind the paint to the wall, and moisture seeping through the brick accelerated the damage. What followed is one of the longest and most desperate conservation efforts in art history.

The Great Restoration

The restoration led by Pinin Brambilla Barcilon from 1978 to 1999 was the most controversial in art conservation history. Working millimeter by millimeter, Barcilon's team removed centuries of overpainting, glue, and varnish that had been applied by previous restorers trying to "fix" Leonardo's work. What they found beneath was both miraculous and heartbreaking.

Only about 20% of Leonardo's original paint survived. But that 20% included some of the most beautiful passages of painting in the entire mural — delicate brushwork, subtle color transitions, and details that had been hidden for centuries. The restoration revealed, for instance, that the sky visible through the windows behind Christ was painted with extraordinary atmospheric perspective, showing clouds receding into the distance.

Critics argued that the restoration had gone too far — that by removing all the overpainting, the team had left the mural looking like a ghost of itself. But defenders maintained that it was the only honest approach: to present what Leonardo actually painted, rather than what later artists imagined he might have painted.

Hidden Details

Despite centuries of damage, the Last Supper still contains extraordinary details that reward close observation. Leonardo used linear perspective with mathematical precision — all the orthogonals of the architecture converge on Christ's right eye, making him the geometric and symbolic center of the composition. The three windows behind the figures form a visual halo around Christ's head.

The food on the table has also been studied extensively. Leonardo painted eel with orange slices — a dish popular at the Sforza court and, coincidentally, a favorite of Ludovico il Moro. Some scholars have suggested this was a subtle tribute to the Duke, while others believe it was simply what Leonardo and the monks were eating during the years of painting.

Visiting Today

Today, the Last Supper is one of the most carefully protected artworks in the world. Visitors enter through a climate-controlled antechamber, and access to the refectory is limited to small groups for 15-minute viewing sessions. The air is filtered and temperature-controlled to prevent further deterioration. Even with these precautions, the mural remains fragile — a fragile miracle that continues to draw hundreds of thousands of visitors each year.
